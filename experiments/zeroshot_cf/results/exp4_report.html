02HELOC: the actionability-plausibility conflict is real at scale

The two HELOC regimes trade the same way the small-sample runs suggested, and the full split makes the trade sharper rather than softer.

From-scratch generates valid, believable applicants. When the beam is free to regenerate every feature, it lands on the target class for 72% of cases and the results sit right on the data manifold: LOF 1.01 against a test-set reference of 1.09, and not a single out-of-bounds cell in 2092 counterfactuals. The catch is in the fine print. These are not edits to the person in front of you. The immutable fields drift by 0.11 on average, so what you get is "a plausible approved applicant", not "this applicant, minimally changed". True actionability is zero by construction.

Frozen keeps the person intact but pays for it twice. Pin the history and age fields to their real values and the counterfactual stops being believable: LOF jumps to 6.5 million. Welding target-class financials onto an applicant's wrong-class credit history produces rows that are inside every individual feature range yet look like no real person. Validity also drops to 0.42. On HELOC the protected fields carry so much of the class signal that holding them fixed fights both goals at once.

03Law: valid and close, once you measure distance correctly

Law is the first dataset here with categorical columns (sex and race, one-hot encoded into ten columns). That broke an assumption I had been carrying, and the correction changed the story.

The beam generates those one-hot columns as continuous values, so I expected garbage: fractional memberships, categories summing to more than one. It does not happen. The autoregressive conditioning plus the range rejection snap them to near-clean one-hots, roughly 0.999 and 0.001, summing to one per group. TabPFN learns the encoding from the context rows.

The distance number needed fixing too. Pure Euclidean over one-hot columns punishes a single category flip by about 1.41, which had inflated Law's proximity to look like a large edit. cel's proximity_l2_jaccard handles this correctly: Euclidean on the three continuous features, Jaccard on the categoricals, weighted by how many of each. Measured properly, Law counterfactuals are small. About 4.4 of 13 features move, the continuous part shifts by only 0.37 in L2, and roughly 24% of category assignments change. This retires the "mode collapse" reading from the earlier small-sample notes. These are modest, individualized edits.

Plausibility is the one soft spot. Law's LOF is 8.7 against a test reference of 1.2, so the counterfactuals sit somewhat outside the dense part of the distribution. Not the catastrophe HELOC-frozen is, but not the near-perfect fit of HELOC from-scratch either.

04The number everyone will misread: validity

The generator reaches the target class almost every time. cel's target_distance is 0.000 for both HELOC from-scratch and Law, which means the oracle's prediction on the counterfactual equals the intended target class for every single instance. So why is validity 0.72 and 0.78 rather than 1.0?

Because cel defines validity against the factual's true label, not the intended target. A counterfactual counts as valid when predict(cf) != y_test. When a factual was already misclassified by the oracle, its intended target equals its true label, so flipping to that target scores as invalid. The HELOC oracle is 72% accurate and the Law oracle 76%, and those accuracies are almost exactly the validity ceilings we see.

This is worth stating plainly because it is easy to report the wrong number. Under the target-reached definition the project used earlier (predict(cf) == y_target), both cells score 1.0. Under cel's standard definition they score at the oracle's accuracy. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. cel asks "did the label actually change from the truth"; the other asks "did we hit the class we aimed for". The gap between them is a property of the oracle, not the generator. It also flags a real design smell: the counterfactuals are generated by TabPFN but judged by a separate LogisticRegression, and the two models disagree on about a quarter of the data before any counterfactual exists.

06How to read this together

If the goal is a plausible target-class example and you do not care about editing a specific person, HELOC from-scratch is the clear winner: valid at the oracle ceiling, indistinguishable from real data, nothing out of bounds. If the goal is a genuine counterfactual that respects what a person cannot change, HELOC frozen is honest about how hard that is on this dataset, and the plausibility collapse it shows is the finding, not a bug to tune away. Law sits in between: small, individualized, valid edits with slightly-off plausibility, and no immutability question because the dataset defines none.

The single most useful takeaway is methodological. Validity depends entirely on which reference you score against, and a separate-oracle setup puts a hard ceiling on it equal to that oracle's accuracy. Any comparison against other counterfactual methods has to fix the oracle and the definition first, or the headline numbers are not talking about the same thing.

Provenance and honest caveats. HELOC from-scratch was generated on a Helios GH200 (CUDA); the other cells were generated earlier on Apple MPS. Expect small numeric drift between backends, not a change in conclusions. The beam settings are fixed across cells (beam width 8, 6 candidates, all-classes context of 256 rows, chunk size 4096, seed 42). Results are not chunk-invariant, so that chunk size is pinned. No external counterfactual baselines (DiCE, PPCEF) were run, so every comparison here is internal to the beam method.
